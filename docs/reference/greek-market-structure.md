# Greek maritime market structure and reputation

Greece is the world's biggest shipowning nation, but most of that fleet does not fly the Greek flag. The strength is in ownership and management, which are run from Piraeus and Athens, not in the registry.

## Size

The Union of Greek Shipowners' 2025–2026 report says Greece remains the world's leading shipping nation, with a fleet of nearly 5,800 vessels, accounting for over 19% of total global tonnage, and Greek shipping represents 61% of the EU-controlled merchant fleet. Clarksons puts it a bit lower, at 17% of the world fleet in dwt terms and more than a fifth of all tankers, bulkers and LNG carriers. The Greek-owned fleet is valued at about $200bn, with more than $75bn of newbuilding orders. For the Greek economy, shipping directly and indirectly accounts for 7-8% of the country's GDP and supports approximately 200,000 jobs.

## How the market is structured

It is almost entirely tramp shipping: bulkers, crude and product tankers, and a growing number of LNG and LPG carriers. Greeks hold a smaller share of container shipping. When they do own container ships, they usually charter them out to liners like MSC or Maersk rather than running liner services themselves.

The companies are mostly family-run. There are a few hundred shipowning groups, from families with 5 ships to groups with 100+ (Angelicoussis, Tsakos, Procopiou, Economou, Martinos, and others). Decisions are made quickly and centrally by the owner or family, not by committees.

Each ship is usually owned by its own company, typically registered in the Marshall Islands, Liberia, Panama or Malta. A Greek management company in Piraeus or Glyfada runs the fleet. That office often does both technical management and commercial management.

The main business model is to own the ships and charter them out. The typical Greek company is a head owner. It fixes its ships on time charter (TCO) or on voyage charter to traders, miners and oil majors. Fewer Greek companies are pure operators that charter in ships and relet them. That matters for your operator focus: pure operators and commodity traders are concentrated more in Geneva, Singapore, London, Hamburg and Copenhagen. Many Greek companies do some operating on top of their owned fleet, though.

The business is based on asset play. Much of the profit historically comes from buying second-hand ships cheap in weak markets and selling or chartering them out when rates go up. Buy/sell timing is often worth more than daily operating margins.

A whole ecosystem sits around Piraeus. It includes brokers, port agents, P&I club correspondents, class society offices (DNV, LR, ABS and the rest), bunker traders, marine insurers, technical suppliers and software vendors. Posidonia, a large shipping exhibition held every two years, is a key industry event. London remains closely linked to Greek shipping for chartering, insurance, arbitration (LMAA) and finance.

The legal and tax setup is favourable to shipowners. There is a tonnage tax regime (tax based on ship size, not profit), and it is protected in the Greek constitution. Law 89/67 lets foreign-flag management offices operate in Greece. This framework is the main reason the management side stays in Greece while the ships fly other flags.

For finance, Greek banks were big lenders before 2008. After the crisis, funding moved to European and Chinese banks, Chinese leasing houses, private credit, and US stock listings (Star Bulk, Danaos, Tsakos, Navios and others).

For crew, officers were traditionally Greek, while ratings (non-officer crew) are mostly Filipino, Indian, Ukrainian and others. The share of Greek officers has dropped over time.

## What the reputation rests on

On the positive side, several things stand out:

- Seafaring tradition going back centuries. Many families come from islands like Chios, Andros and Kefalonia.
- Entrepreneurial risk-taking and good market timing: buying against the cycle and moving fast.
- Resilience. Greek companies came through the 1980s crash and the 2008–2016 downturn, and many expanded during bad years.
- Trust and personal relationships. Deals often rely on phone calls and long-standing broker relationships.
- Now, fleet renewal. There is heavy investment in eco and dual-fuel newbuildings, and the Greek fleet is no longer the old, second-hand fleet it was known for decades ago.

On the critical side:

- The historical image of owning older, cheaper tonnage, though this has largely changed.
- Controversy over Greek owners carrying Russian oil after 2022 under the price cap. Some older tankers were also sold to buyers linked to the "dark fleet".
- The flag-of-convenience structure. It is legal, but it gets criticism on transparency and tax.
- Slower adoption of digital tools compared with Nordic or Singapore operators. Mid-size Greek firms often still use Excel plus basic systems. A company's size largely decides whether it runs IMOS or a local or in-house system.

If you're looking at this as a software market: the Greek opportunity is mainly owner-managers who need technical, commercial and emissions tools (CII, EU ETS, FuelEU). Pure voyage-operator tools like IMOS fit better with the bigger groups and with the non-Greek operator and trader hubs.
